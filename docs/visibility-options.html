Drawn or not — six ways

Each panel shows the same four rows: two layers drawn, two hidden. Squint at each one and see how long it takes to say which two are off.

The test that matters is not whether the control looks good when you are pointing at it. It is whether you can read the state of twenty rows at a glance, without hunting — and whether the control still reads as a control, so nobody wonders what happens if they click it.

1 · Switch (what you have)

A track and a knob. Unmistakably a control, and the accent fill is easy to scan — but it is 24 pixels of the 296 the row has, spent on a binary.

2 · The symbol is the switch

No separate control at all. Click the layer's own swatch to draw or hide it; a hidden layer's symbol goes grey and dashed, like a footprint with nothing in it.

Against: it says the state in words, which cannot be misread, but it costs the space the counts and classes were using — and words are slower to scan than shapes.

I would take 5. The reason the switch is not working is not that a switch is ugly; it is that it was the only thing carrying the state. One signal in a 24-pixel box, on a row 44 pixels tall, next to a symbol that looked identical whether the layer was drawn or not.

5 puts four signals on it — the icon changes shape, the colour drops out, the whole row dims, and a hatch runs behind it — so the answer arrives before you have focused on anything. The dimming and the greyed symbol do most of the work, which is why the icon can go back to being an eye: the metaphor everybody already has for "drawn on the map", and the one QGIS, ArcGIS and every web map library uses.

4 is the safe answer and I would not argue with it. A checkbox is what a GIS user reaches for and nobody has ever had to learn it. If you would rather the panel look like a professional tool than like a phone settings screen, take 4 and keep the row dimming from 5.
